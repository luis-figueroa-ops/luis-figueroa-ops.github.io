Building the Operational Foundation for Verizon Business’s I2B Channel

The I2B Channel’s Backstory

Before it was formalized, the Authorized Retailer Business channel lacked dedicated Verizon Business infrastructure, standardized reporting, and a defined commission-support model. Much of the underlying partner sales and transaction data resided within Verizon Consumer Group systems, while Verizon Business had limited direct access and often relied on manual information requests. With reporting warehouses and data sources maintained separately across the two organizations, the channel operated without a unified Business-side view of performance, payout eligibility, or operational activity.

That changed when Verizon Business leadership moved to formally establish the channel as an official SMB sales motion. The initiative, known internally as Agent Transformation, brought the Authorized Retailer Business channel into Verizon Business’s broader channel structure to expand market reach, accelerate growth opportunities, and create a more scalable operating model.

As the channel was formalized, Verizon Business built the operational foundation required to support and scale it. Workstreams progressed simultaneously across Sales, Commissions, reporting, operational support, and cross-functional Business teams. A parallel reporting and reconciliation process improved visibility into partner activity and commission administration, while broader efforts established clearer ownership, more consistent processes, and the backend support model needed to operate the channel as part of Verizon Business.

At the same time, teams reverse-engineered the existing Retail to Business (R2B) model as an operational reference point for Indirect to Business (I2B). The goal was not to duplicate R2B exactly, but to adapt its proven capabilities - sales tools, reporting, support pathways, process governance, and partner enablement - to the distinct needs of the Authorized Retailer channel. This created a more consistent Business-selling and support experience across Exclusive Authorized Retailers, including organizations such as Victra, TCC, Wireless Zone, CSOK, and other partner groups.

The result was a channel that increasingly operated within the same Verizon Business ecosystem as other Business sales groups: connected to core systems, supported by cross-functional teams, and equipped with more consistent processes for sales activity, order management, service support, financial operations, and performance management. Rather than depending on manual workarounds or account managers as intermediaries for routine needs, partner agents gained clearer direct pathways to the tools, data, and support required to serve business customers and grow the channel.

The section below details just a few of my contributions to this transformation, including the cross-functional work used to expand channel access, strengthen operational controls, enable partner teams, and integrate new tools and processes into the Verizon Business environment.

Securing Prospecting Tool Access for the Partner Channel

Key Results
  • Increased funnel productivity 20% in four months across our partner sales channel by securing the same prospecting tool our direct channel used for 90+ internal account managers and 8,000+ external agent sellers, after turning the tool's Senior Manager from a firm no into a sponsor.

Answers: Tell me about a time you had to align several stakeholders to get a firm no turned into a yes.

SituationAs we formalized the Indirect-to-Business (I2B) partner channel, part of the buildout was finding the tools our direct channel, Retail-to-Business (R2B), already used and getting I2B access wherever Legal and Compliance allowed. List builder, a Salesforce-integrated prospecting tool, was one of those gaps. R2B sellers used it to build filtered, prioritized lead lists, while the channel's 90+ internal account managers and 8,000+ external agent sellers built theirs by hand, which put them at a disadvantage before the first customer conversation.

Obstacle / ObjectiveObstacle: Not every tool could cross over. Some were off-limits to a third-party channel for legal or compliance reasons, and the ones that could came with restrictions, so a "no" was a real possibility. When I brought the business case forward, the list builder Senior Manager gave one: the channel was third party, and that alone was reason enough, with no exception process. Getting to yes meant satisfying the tool's leadership, Salesforce, Legal, Risk and Compliance, and Marketing Science, none of whom reported to me. And even with approval, external agents couldn't be required to use the tool, so adoption would have to be built without a mandate.

Objective: Give the partner channel the same prospecting advantage R2B already had, so sellers could find and win more business customers and grow gross adds, the new lines and customers that drive channel growth.

ActionMy approach to a no like this is to find the risk the person is protecting against, then prove whether it's real.

Turning a no into a question: I treated the Senior Manager's objection as a claim to verify rather than a decision to argue. His priority wasn't blocking the tool, it was making sure a third-party agent couldn't expose the company. So I reframed the request in his terms: not "give us access," but "here's the proof this doesn't create the risk you're protecting against," and researched the agreement language on third-party restrictions myself. That turned a closed door into a question we both wanted answered.

Aligning the stakeholders: From there, I brought in each team whose concern stood between the request and a yes:

  • Salesforce leadership, who would enable the tool for the channel
  • Legal
  • Risk and Compliance
  • Marketing Science, who could confirm how customers on the national do-not-call registry were handled

Proving the risk wasn't there: Working through each team's specific concern in turn, each one proved solvable. Legal's restriction on which device types could appear, for example, turned out to be a configuration setting rather than a blocker. Once every concern cleared, there was nothing left to say no to.

Equipping the field: With approval in place, the challenge shifted from permission to adoption. I partnered with Learning & Development to equip the field and reached external sellers through contacts at each agent company to drive adoption internally, since I had no direct line to them. Tracking adoption through reporting, rather than enforcement, kept uptake visible without a mandate.

ResultThe research came back clean: no third-party restriction existed, and the Senior Manager who had shut the request down became its sponsor. For the first time, I2B account managers and external sellers worked from the same filtered, prioritized prospect lists as R2B, closing one of the tool gaps the channel buildout set out to fix. Adoption held without a mandate, and the channel's funnel productivity rose in the months that followed.

The pattern I keep noticing: most hard "no's" aren't actually about the request, they're about who's on the hook if it goes wrong. That's where Sales Operations and project management meet: the work in between the ask and the yes, owned from business case to adoption.

Data-Driven Budget and Program Governance

Key Results
  • Reduced equipment discount spend $750K/yr on a $5M budget, gave leadership its first agent- and program-level view of profitability, and automated approvals up to $100 ahead of the roadmap, while making agent-owned budgets work under controls that met Risk's audit requirements.

Answers: Tell me about a time you had to change a process that was deeply ingrained in how the organization worked driving business results.

SituationThe $5M annual equipment discount budget I managed for our partner sales channel funded targeted discounts that helped sellers win harder-to-close business customers. It covered multiple programs, tracked as a single pot across several spreadsheets, and the limits were never enforced: when spending ran over, more funding was requested from Finance, which rarely said no. When I took over the program, I started consolidating that data into one view, and a few months later, an SVP making changes across the organization asked whether this spend was actually profitable, and nothing in place could answer that.

Opportunity / Obstacle / ObjectiveOpportunity: When I walked sales leadership through the data I'd need to collect to answer that question, the Sales Sr. Director saw a bigger fix: remove his internal Sr. Account Managers from tracking agent spend and hand that responsibility to the external agent partners themselves. The Client Partner Sr. Manager and her team agreed that agents should own their budgets.

Obstacle: No model existed for agent ownership: no way for agents to submit requests, no agent-level allocations, and no controls. Agents couldn't use the company's Google Workspace for compliance reasons, and Risk wouldn't approve the shift until we could audit spending we no longer directly controlled. If we got it wrong, Finance and support teams faced processing delays, reps faced confusion, and customers felt it.

Objective: Keep the discounts doing their job, helping sellers win the business customers who could get a better deal elsewhere and driving gross adds (new lines and customers), while giving leadership a budget it could audit and measure for profitability.

ActionMy approach to a redesign like this is to find the real constraint before designing the fix, then build controls the people using them won't need to work around.

Finding the real constraint: Before redesigning anything, I traced the overspending to its structural cause: internal account managers were tracking spend on agents' behalf, which left agents with no visibility into their own budgets. I flagged the overspend and processing-delay risk to leadership before launch, so the redesign started from the cause, not the symptom.

Designing for the people using it: Because Google Workspace was off-limits to agents, I taught myself Smartsheet and designed a submission process agents could reach directly, supported by SOPs and field-facing decks. For the first time, agents had one place to request funds and track spend against their own allocation.

Closing the audit gap with Risk and IT: Risk's concern was fair: how do you audit spending you don't control once the Sr. Account Managers step out? Reviewing every order by hand wasn't realistic at that volume, so I partnered with IT, who could pull the actual device tied to each submitted phone number, and verified every submission against that source data. The real value was the audit trail: a working control behind a form that stayed quick for agents, which cleared Risk to approve the change.

Turning submissions into an answer: Because each partner company carried its own budget, I built an Excel dashboard breaking spend out by agent, program, and device category against each allocation. That gave Finance and leadership what the SVP had actually asked for: not just how much was spent, but whether each program, and each agent, was profitable.

Keeping relationships steady: Client Partners owned the relationships with agent leadership, so I kept them aligned on the strategy and rollout while building in controls that protected the budget. Holding back 10% of each allocation as a contingency gave agents a path to request more for larger opportunities, without going back to Finance for incremental funding.

Automating what the system allowed: Automation wasn't in scope until later in the channel's integration, and point-of-sale automation above $100 wasn't feasible yet, but waiting would have kept every request on manual review. My Director and I decided to automate approvals up to $100, and I partnered with the dedicated Agent Transformation Sr. Manager on my team to gather the requirements, so smaller requests were approved automatically while larger ones stayed in the controlled workflow.

ResultFor the first time, leadership could see the budget by agent and program, and answer whether the spend was paying off. Agents managed their own budgets with their own teams instead of relying on account managers to track spend for them, and overspending came down. Requests up to $100 were approved automatically, a capability that hadn't been planned until later. Client Partners kept their agent relationships steady through a change that directly affected those agents' money, and my dashboard became the view my Director and Sr. Director used directly, feeding Finance's AVP and SVP readouts. Months later, that same visibility showed how confusing the programs had become for sales reps, which led the AVP to restructure the program on a reporting foundation built for further automation.

A budget can only be managed if it can be measured, and only owned if the process fits the people using it. That's where Sales Operations and governance meet: building the controls that turn spending into answers leadership can act on, without adding friction for the people doing the work.

Owning Issue Resolution

Key Results
  • Cut monthly field escalations 50% within about eight months for 90+ internal account managers supporting 8,000+ external agent sellers, by turning scattered requests into shared visibility and fixing recurring causes with the support teams that owned them.

Answers: Tell me about a time you took ownership of a recurring problem and got multiple teams to address it at the source.

SituationIn our partner sales channel, a peer and I were the two Sr. Analysts handling operational support for the entire national channel: 90+ internal account managers and the 8,000+ external agent sellers they supported. The account managers fielded the sellers' system, credit, and porting issues and escalated what they couldn't solve, about 200 to 300 requests a month, through a shared inbox, direct emails, and Slack messages to whoever might know the answer. Every escalation was time away from selling, and often a transaction stuck until someone fixed it.

Obstacle / ObjectiveObstacle: Account managers couldn't see each other's troubleshooting, so an issue one person had already solved got investigated again from scratch. Nothing was captured as data, so patterns lived only in individual memory, and each support function owned a piece of the failure points without anyone seeing the whole. With two of us covering the channel, answering the same questions one at a time couldn't keep up.

Objective: Reduce recurring escalations by making their root causes visible and fixing them at the source, whether that meant correcting a rep-side habit, fixing a process, or bringing in Operations or IT, so reps spent less time troubleshooting and more time selling.

ActionMy approach to recurring problems is to make them visible first, so the conversation shifts from the request in front of us to the pattern behind it.

Building buy-in first: Before building anything, I floated a single, standardized Slack intake channel at our monthly All Hands, and the account managers backed it on the spot, speaking up and posting their support in the meeting chat. Once it was live, I partnered with my Senior Director to retire the old submission form, since that call had to come from leadership. That gave the change the field's support and leadership's backing from day one.

Making every issue visible: Because the channel was shared, account managers could see each other's questions and Ops' answers as they happened, and many found their answer before posting or chimed in when they were seeing the same issue. Behind it, I captured every request as structured data and reviewed the patterns monthly, which turned one-off messages into categories with clear owners.

Fixing root causes at the source: With the data grouped by category, I took each recurring pattern to the teams that could fix it, most often billing, finance, fraud, porting, credit, payments, and write-off, plus IT when the cause was a system issue. Our monthly stakeholder calls ran in both directions: support teams raised issues the field was causing, and the field's data showed where support processes needed correcting, including coaching reps when the fix was on their end. While backend fixes were pending, I found workarounds so no one was left stuck.

ResultRecurring issues started getting fixed where they began instead of resurfacing month after month, and escalations steadily declined. Account managers stopped re-asking questions a peer had already solved, because the answer was already in the shared channel, and the back-and-forth there educated the field faster than one-off replies could. Support teams and the field finally shared a view of what was actually driving escalations, so conversations centered on root causes rather than the single request in front of them.

Most escalations aren't new problems, they're the same few problems showing up under different names. That's where Sales Operations and process improvement meet: fixing the pattern, not just the ticket in front of you.

Realigning Four Organizations After a Reorg

Key Results
  • Maintained continuity of customer credits through the reorg by helping four teams successfully adapt to new roles and run a shared process serving 6,000+ monthly submissions, eliminating recurring Tier 3 escalations.

Answers: Tell me about a time you helped multiple teams adapt to a major organizational change.

SituationAt the end of Q1 2022, a company-wide reorg eliminated the team that handled promo corrections for the 8,000+ external agent sellers in our Indirect-to-Business (I2B) partner channel. Promo corrections fix promotional credits a customer was promised but didn't receive. A newly formed Center of Excellence on the Consumer side was set up to absorb that team's work, but not its Business-side corrections. That would have left all 6,000+ monthly submissions with the two Sr. Analysts supporting the entire national channel: my peer and me. My manager and Sr. Director negotiated with Center of Excellence leadership to take on the Business-side work, with my team supporting it, and I volunteered to lead that support. The stakes were real. When corrections stalled, customers missed credits, billing disputes grew, agents lost trust and sales, and 90+ internal account managers lost strategy and site-visit time to escalations.

Obstacle / ObjectiveObstacle: An agreed handoff still had to become a process four groups could run together, and none of them had worked together on this before. Agent partners needed a new way to submit. Center of Excellence reps were new to the Business side and cautious because of it. Finance operations and billing operations had to fit a new intake into workflows already in motion. The hardest cases, the Tier 3 escalations, still came to my peer and me. This queue was separate from the Ops Support Slack channel we also ran, and resolving each case meant waiting on finance operations, sometimes for days.

Objective: Keep customer credits flowing and protect agents' time and relationships by turning the reorg-driven handoff into a sustainable process the four groups could successfully operate together.

ActionWhen a change hands work to people who have never done it, I start with what each group needs to succeed in its new role, then remove the friction between them.

Building the Business side into the design: The Center of Excellence owned the process and form design, since Consumer-side agents shared the same form. I partnered with them to make sure Business requirements were built in from the start. I also showed them which downstream team handled each promo type, billing operations or finance operations. As a result, cases reached the right team the first time instead of bouncing between queues.

Equipping a cautious new team: The Center of Excellence reps' hesitation came from unfamiliarity with the Business side, not resistance. I addressed it by getting them Salesforce access for the promo types that required it, then coaching them on how finance operations handled each type and how to choose the correct Business workflow. I backed that with documentation for every audience, from a leadership overview to rep-level steps. Within about one to two months, the reps were routing cases confidently on their own.

Surfacing the gap neither side could see: Trade-in promos kept getting denied and resubmitted, and the back-and-forth was clogging both teams' queues without anyone knowing why. I brought the finance operations leader together with the Center of Excellence Sr. Analyst and her leader, and we walked through both processes side by side. That revealed they were confirming device receipt against different data. Drawing on what I knew from an earlier role, I proposed that reps reply to the case's automated confirmation email with a screenshot of the store's receipt. I confirmed the option still worked, and finance operations agreed to accept it. That ended the back-and-forth.

Designing around a stakeholder's preference: Tier 3 cases still waited on emails to the finance operations Sr. Manager, who preferred that her team not be emailed directly. Instead of pushing for direct access, I proposed a shared Google Sheet. The Center of Excellence's Tier 2 contact logged each case, and finance reps updated the notes. That removed the wait and the middle step. My peer and I stayed in the loop by design, but only for channel-level issues or when an account manager needed to coach an agent.

ResultPromo corrections had clear owners again, with the Center of Excellence, finance operations, and billing operations running them as one connected process. The hardest cases were resolved at Tier 2 instead of reaching my peer and me. Over time, the Center of Excellence's own metrics showed fewer credits issued through corrections and fewer submissions per agent company, which pointed to agents getting cases right the first time. Agents and the 90+ account managers had a clear path for customer issues, and the national support layer was freed for work that needed it.

A reorg can move work to a new team overnight, but a process only works once every group knows its part. That's where Sales Operations and change management meet: turning a handoff on paper into a process the people involved can actually run.

Resolving a Salesforce Lead Routing Gap

Key Results
  • Recovered 40+ misrouted sales leads a month for external agent sellers, protecting new-line KPI growth, partner commissions, and customer relationships until the permanent system fix I advocated for replaced the manual workaround.

Answers: Tell me about a time you recognized how a system problem was affecting people and helped another team understand their side.

SituationOur dot-com initiative routed self-service orders back to the local agent partner. When a customer ordered through the company's online business portal, the partner's seller could still follow up and earn the commission, so self-service wouldn't cut sellers out of their own business. The Salesforce team came to me after noticing a growing backlog of these leads sitting unassigned in our Indirect-to-Business (I2B) partner channel, where I was one of two Sr. Analysts supporting the entire national channel. In our direct channel, leads that couldn't reach the assigned rep moved up to the manager and director, but that fallback wasn't working for the partner channel. Every unassigned lead put a commission opportunity, a potential gross add (the new lines and customers that drive channel growth), and a customer relationship at risk, since a customer who never heard from their local rep might call in, deal with someone else in a store, or switch carriers.

Obstacle / ObjectiveObstacle: The Salesforce team understood the routing logic but not how the partner channel was structured, and agent titles weren't standardized across retailers the way internal titles were. A permanent fix needed funding for a system enhancement that wasn't a priority yet. That meant the gap had to be held manually without letting the fix quietly fall off the list.

Objective: Keep every lead reaching the right agent partner, so new-line growth, partner commissions, and customer relationships stayed protected until the system could route them on its own.

ActionWhen a system gap affects people outside the team that owns the system, I start by making sure both sides see the same problem, then protect the people affected while the real fix gets built.

Translating the partner channel for the Salesforce team: I walked the Salesforce team through how the partner channel was structured, and together we saw that agent titles weren't standardized across retailers. When internal title changes didn't carry through to the systems the partner channel relied on, leads lost their path to the right seller. That shared understanding turned a growing backlog into a defined gap the Salesforce team could scope a fix for.

Protecting the leads in the meantime: When the Salesforce team confirmed a permanent fix needed funding, I set up a manual safety net rather than letting the leads wait. For about a year, I reviewed the backlog three times a week and partnered with the Client Partner team, who had the access to reassign each lead to the right agent partner. That kept the leads reaching sellers while there was still time to follow up with the customer.

Keeping the permanent fix a priority: A manual process can quietly become the permanent one, so I kept the enhancement visible with the Salesforce team. I tied the leads to gross adds and new lines, both company KPIs, so the gap read as a business risk rather than a team inconvenience. Once it was prioritized, the enhancement shipped in a release alongside other Salesforce fixes.

ResultSelf-service leads reached the right agent partner automatically, or moved up the partner's hierarchy when needed, the same way they already did in the direct channel. Sellers kept their chance to follow up and earn the commission, customers heard from their local rep, and the manual review was retired.

A manual workaround is fine as a bridge, as long as someone is building the case to replace it. That's where Sales Operations and business systems meet: holding the gap by hand until the system can hold it on its own.

Coordinating the POS Migration

Key Results
  • Prepared a third-party channel of 8,000+ external agent sellers for a mandatory point-of-sale cutover by aligning agent companies and the design team through 40+ testing sessions, so the system fit how partner stores actually work.

Answers: Tell me about a time you had to align people from different groups to make a new system work for the people who would use it.

SituationThe company moved every frontline team, including field, in-store, telesales, door-to-door, and customer service, onto one new point-of-sale (POS) system built to cut clicks, speed up revenue capture, and improve the customer experience. Instead of building the tool and pushing mandatory training, the design team asked a sales operations representative from each channel to help shape the rollout, because each channel sold differently and the system would be customized for each. I represented our Indirect-to-Business (I2B) partner channel: 8,000+ external agent sellers in third-party authorized retail stores, the outside counterpart to our direct channel, Retail-to-Business (R2B). The switch was mandatory, so if the system didn't fit how those stores worked, sellers would find out in front of a customer.

Obstacle / ObjectiveObstacle: As one of two Sr. Analysts supporting the entire national partner channel, I had no direct line to the store reps or their managers. Every rep had to come through a contact at their agent company, a partner I didn't manage. The design team was also building from how internal sellers worked, so differences in how partner stores operated could easily be missed.

Objective: Make sure partner sellers could complete transactions confidently from day one on a version of the system that fit how their stores worked, so the switch didn't slow sales or hurt the customer experience.

ActionWhen a tool is being built for many groups at once, I focus on making sure the group I represent is understood, not just included.

Aligning the agent companies first: Since I couldn't reach store reps directly, I worked through a contact at each agent company. For each testing call I sent the date, time, and conference link and asked them to choose reps who were willing to join. Over the year-long rollout, that grew into 40+ structured sessions with about 50 reps on each, which gave the design team steady access to the sellers who would use the system.

Catching what the design missed: On one call, an agent mentioned liking the new device guarantee button. That made me ask the design team whether the partner channel's existing return process had carried over. It hadn't. The button was built the way internal sellers handled returns, but partner stores owned their own inventory and ran returns through their own process. I walked design through the difference, and they built a separate refund and exchange option for the partner channel. That way, the system matched how those stores actually worked.

Equipping reps to practice on their own: Once the design was final, I created quick-reference guides that walked reps back through the scenarios they had tested and showed how their feedback had shaped the system. Reps could then keep building familiarity between calls, not just during them.

Making the parallel window count: Both systems stayed live for about a year by company design. My part was making sure our channel used that time. The testing calls and guides kept reps working in the new system throughout the year rather than waiting for the shutoff.

ResultWhen the old system shut off for every channel at once, partner sellers moved over on a version built around how their stores worked. They were already familiar with it instead of discovering the gaps in front of a customer. Agent companies that sat outside the company's systems had helped shape the tool their sellers would use every day.

One system can serve many teams, but only if each team's way of working is in the room when it's built. That's where Sales Operations and field enablement meet: making sure the people using a tool help shape it before they're required to use it.

Closing a Territory Data Access Gap

Key Results
  • Eliminated commission delays of up to two months for event locations by getting them into the correct payout month, while improving visibility into standard-door changes ahead of the T-8 cutoff and reducing territory and quota discrepancies 16%.

Answers: Tell me about a time you worked with other teams and discovered that a complaint had more than one cause.

SituationA Sales Associate Director raised a concern about Indirect Business event locations, virtual outlets that could be activated at any point during the month. The Consumer Division maintained the partner location records, but the Business side had limited visibility into when these locations went live. By the time Business learned about an activation, reps could face a wait of up to two months for commissions on the new line orders tied to that location.

ObstacleWhat first appeared to be one commission complaint involved two different gaps. Event locations did not appear on the standard location report, so the Business side needed another way to learn when they became active and get them to Commissions promptly. Standard doors presented a different challenge: store assignments and changes still had to follow the Business channel’s existing T-8 deadline, eight days before month-end, to be reflected in the next month’s payout. The Consumer Division held the underlying partner location data, while the Business side was responsible for its own territory and commission-related work without the same timely access to that data. A submission after T-8 could therefore miss the next payout month. I needed to address both gaps without replacing the formal Salesforce Chatter intake or treating every location change as something my tracking process could capture.

ActionBringing the right teams together: I set up calls with the Consumer Division, which maintained the partner location data. Working through the event-location complaint together revealed that event locations and standard doors were reaching the commission process in different ways. I separated the issues so we could address each on its own terms rather than apply one workaround to both.

Getting event locations paid on time: Because event locations could go live at any point in the month and did not appear on the standard report, I worked out a direct arrangement with Commissions. When an automated email from the Consumer Division notified me that an event location was active, I entered it into Commissions’ spreadsheet immediately. That backend step got the location into the correct payout month. Reps still submitted their information through the normal Salesforce Chatter process for Territory Management; my step did not replace that intake.

Giving standard doors earlier visibility: For standard doors, the T-8 deadline remained in place. I secured formal cross-divisional access to the Consumer Division’s Tableau report and built a tracking approach that fed location information into the recurring sheet my Territory Management partner already maintained. That gave Territory Management and Area Directors earlier notice of new and closed locations so they could work on assignments before the cutoff. It provided additional visibility, not a complete record of every new or closed door, and I did not manage rep-to-rep assignment changes through it.

Reinforcing the submission deadline: I also went back to reps to explain why submitting ahead of T-8 mattered for the next payout month. That guidance supported the existing Salesforce Chatter process; it was not the fix for the Business side’s limited access to location data.

ResultEvent locations reached Commissions as soon as I received the activation notice, so reps were paid in the correct month instead of waiting up to two months. For standard doors, Territory Management gained earlier visibility to work against the existing T-8 deadline. Territory and quota discrepancies recorded through my Ops Support Slack channel and backend Google Sheet declined 16%. Sales, Territory Management, and Commissions had a clearer way to handle their respective parts of the issue without treating event locations and standard doors as the same problem.

Complaints often arrive as one problem, even when there are two underneath. That's where Sales Operations and problem diagnosis meet: separating the causes so each gets the fix it needs.

Driving AI Adoption Across the Channel

Key Results

Reps were also experimenting with AI on their own and wanted to understand what the Salesforce tool could do now and what might make it more useful later. Automating Quota Relief for Weather-Closed Stores

Key Results
  • Reduced weather-related quota-relief escalations in the Ops Support Slack channel by 29% by turning a manual, deadline-driven calculation into an automated workflow. The workflow covered 90+ account managers, removing the need for them to remember or calculate the adjustment themselves.

Answers: Tell me about a time you translated business needs into technical requirements.

SituationWhen severe weather closed a store, the account managers assigned to it could need quota relief because they had fewer selling days. Without that adjustment, their quota attainment could appear lower than it should, affecting their payout. But there was no systematic process for calculating or submitting the relief. Because closures were infrequent, an account manager who hadn’t been taught the process - or simply forgot to do it - might not discover the problem until their payout was wrong and they needed an escalation to correct it. As their sales operations analyst, I had been handling the calculation manually so they wouldn’t have to carry that burden.

ObstacleThe manual process meant pulling the relevant Tableau reports, exporting them to Excel, using VLOOKUPs and pivot tables to identify affected account managers and stores, calculating the relief percentage, and getting the result to Finance before the adjustment deadline. It worked, but it depended on me repeating every step correctly and on time whenever a closure occurred. A wrong report, mismatched lookup, or miscalculated percentage could carry through to Finance, while a missed handoff could delay the adjustment altogether.

ActionMapping the process: Before bringing the work to IT, I mapped it end to end: the reports I pulled, the Excel calculations I performed, the business rules behind them, and the handoff Finance needed. That gave IT more than a request to “automate quota relief.” It gave them the logic and expected output of a process I had already been performing manually.

Translating the business rules: I showed IT the Tableau reports I used and walked them through how I combined the data in Excel to identify closed stores, find the account managers assigned to them, and calculate each relief percentage. I explained that a closure appearing on the report counted as one closure in my calculation, regardless of whether it was partial or full-day. That gave IT the data sources, calculation logic, and expected output they needed to automate the process and email the results to Finance and me.

Validating before go-live: I compared the workflow’s results with my manual Excel calculations to check that it produced the expected relief. Once validated, the workflow handled the data retrieval and calculation and emailed the relief information to Finance and me.

ResultAccount managers no longer had to know about, remember, or perform an infrequent adjustment to avoid a closure-driven quota and payout issue. The calculation and Finance handoff became a repeatable workflow instead of a deadline-dependent manual task. Weather-related quota-relief escalations in the Ops Support Slack channel fell by 29%.

The business need wasn’t simply a faster spreadsheet. It was a reliable way to handle an adjustment people could easily forget. That’s where Sales Operations meets business analysis: translating the manual work and its rules into a process that runs without depending on someone remembering every step.
